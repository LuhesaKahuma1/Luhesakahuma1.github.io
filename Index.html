How to spot a phishing message

Phishing is a message that pretends to come from someone you trust, such as your bank, mobile money provider, employer or a friend, to trick you into giving up a password, PIN or money. It arrives by SMS, WhatsApp, email or a phone call.

Warning signs

Pressure and urgency. “Your account will be closed in one hour.” Real organizations give you time and several ways to confirm. Scammers want you to act before you think.

Requests for secrets. No genuine bank, mobile money agent or support team will ask for your PIN, password or one-time code. If someone asks, it is a scam, even if they know your name.

Strange links. Look closely at the web address. A link like “mybank-secure-login.example.com” is not the bank’s real domain. When unsure, do not tap the link. Open the official app or type the address yourself.

Too-good-to-be-true offers. Free money, surprise prizes and job offers that ask for a fee up front are classic bait.

What to do

Stop and do not reply. Contact the organization using a number you already trust. Report the message to your network provider or bank. If you already shared a password, change it at once and turn on two-step login. If you shared a PIN, contact your provider immediately.

Passwords that are strong and easy to remember

A strong password is long and unique. Length matters more than clever symbols. Reusing one password everywhere is the biggest risk, because when one website leaks it, attackers try that password on your email and social accounts.

Use a passphrase

Join four or five unrelated words, for example “river-lamp-orange-drum-seven”. It is easy to remember and very hard to guess. Avoid names, birthdays, phone numbers and common words like “password” or “123456”.

One password per important account

Your email deserves its own password above all, because email can reset almost every other account. Protect it first.

Turn on two-step login

Two-step login (also called 2FA) asks for a code in addition to your password. Even if someone steals the password, they are stopped. Use an authenticator app when possible, as SMS codes are weaker.

Keep track safely

A reputable password manager can store many passwords so you only remember one strong passphrase. If you prefer paper, keep it at home in a safe place, never photographed on your phone.

Never share your password or a login code with anyone, including people who claim to be support staff.

Seven steps to secure your Android phone

Your phone holds your money, messages and photos. These steps take about ten minutes and use only built-in settings.

1. Set a screen lock

Use a PIN of six digits or more, or a strong pattern. Add a fingerprint for convenience.

2. Update your phone and apps

Updates fix known security holes. Turn on automatic updates in the Play Store and in system settings.

3. Install apps only from the Play Store

Apps from unknown websites or shared files are the most common way phones get infected. Check the developer name and reviews before installing.

4. Review app permissions

A flashlight app does not need your contacts. In Settings, open Permissions and remove anything an app does not need.

5. Turn on Find My Device

If your phone is lost or stolen, you can locate it, lock it or erase it remotely.

6. Back up your data

Back up photos and contacts to your Google account so a lost phone does not mean lost memories.

7. Be careful on public Wi-Fi

Avoid banking or mobile money on open Wi-Fi networks. Mobile data is safer.

Ransomware: how it locks your files and what to do

Ransomware is malicious software that encrypts your files and demands money to unlock them. It usually arrives through a fake attachment, a pirated program or a link in a message, and it can stop a student, a shop or a clinic from working in minutes.

Warning signs and defence

Warning signs include files that suddenly will not open, strange new file extensions, and a note on screen demanding payment. The best defence is a recent backup kept away from your computer, such as a cloud copy or an external drive that is unplugged after use. Keep your system updated and never run files from sources you do not trust. If you are hit, disconnect from Wi-Fi and cables straight away so it cannot spread, and do not rely on paying: there is no guarantee you will get your files back.

Restore from your backup after cleaning the device, and change your passwords from a clean device.

SIM swap fraud: how scammers take over your number

In a SIM swap, a scammer convinces your network provider to move your phone number to a SIM they control. They then receive your calls, messages and one-time codes, which can open the door to your mobile money and social media accounts.

Warning signs and defence

The usual warning sign is your phone suddenly losing signal for no reason, followed by alerts about logins or resets you did not make. Protect yourself by never sharing your ID number, PIN or one-time codes, and by asking your provider about adding a PIN or password to your SIM account. Prefer an authenticator app over SMS codes where you can. If the signal drops unexpectedly, contact your provider from another phone immediately and warn your mobile money provider.

Speed matters: the sooner the swap is reversed, the less the scammer can take.

DDoS attacks: when a website is flooded

A DDoS (distributed denial of service) attack sends huge amounts of traffic to a website or online service from many infected devices at once. The service becomes slow or unavailable, so real customers cannot reach it. It is usually about disruption, not stealing data.

Warning signs and defence

Signs include a sudden slowdown, pages timing out and traffic spikes from unusual places. A small business can reduce the risk by using a hosting provider or content delivery network with built-in DDoS protection, setting rate limits, and watching traffic alerts. Keep logs, because they help identify the pattern, and have a plan for how to tell customers if the site is down. Also keep your own devices updated, so they are not recruited into someone else’s attack.

Your own phones and routers can be part of such a network if they are infected, which is another reason to update and use strong passwords.

1. Digital Forensics and Digital Evidence Fundamentals

Digital forensics is the science of identifying, preserving, analysing and presenting data from digital devices so it can be used as reliable evidence. It supports criminal cases, corporate investigations, incident response and civil disputes. Whenever a computer, phone, server, camera or network device is involved in an event, there is usually a digital trail, and forensics is the discipline that finds that trail, explains it and defends it.

Branches of digital forensics

The field is wide. Computer forensics examines laptops, desktops and servers. Mobile forensics deals with phones and tablets. Network forensics studies traffic and logs, while memory forensics analyses what is running in RAM. Cloud forensics looks at data held in online services, and malware forensics studies malicious code. Each branch uses different tools, but all share the same principles of preservation, documentation and objectivity.

The investigation process

Most investigations follow clear phases. First comes identification: deciding which devices and data sources may hold relevant information. Next is preservation, where the scene is secured so nothing is changed or lost. Collection follows, using forensically sound methods to make copies. Then the examiner moves to examination and analysis, searching the copies for relevant artifacts and interpreting what they mean. Finally the findings are written into a report and, if needed, presented in court or to management.

The golden rule is never to work on the original evidence. Every action is documented in notes so that another examiner could repeat the steps and reach the same result. This repeatability is what separates forensic work from casual troubleshooting.

What counts as digital evidence

Digital evidence is any information stored or transmitted in digital form that can support or disprove a claim. Examples include documents, spreadsheets, emails, chat messages, photos, videos, browser history, system logs, application data and the metadata attached to all of these. Metadata, meaning data about data, such as creation dates, authors, GPS coordinates and device identifiers, often tells a story that the visible content does not, so it is always preserved together with the file.

Volatile and non-volatile evidence

Evidence is classed by how long it survives. Volatile evidence, such as RAM contents, running processes, open network connections and logged-in users, disappears when the device loses power. Non-volatile evidence, such as hard drives, SSDs, USB drives and memory cards, remains after shutdown. Because volatile data is lost first, investigators follow the order of volatility: collect the most fragile evidence first (memory and network state), then temporary files, then disk data, then logs and backups.

Characteristics of good evidence

For evidence to be accepted it must be authentic (it is what it claims to be), reliable (collected and processed with trusted methods), complete (not selectively presented), and believable to a court. It also must be admissible, which means it was obtained lawfully. Examiners need proper authority such as a warrant, a court order or written consent, and must respect privacy by examining only what the case requires.

Why digital evidence is fragile

Digital data is easy to change, copy and delete, often without leaving obvious signs. Simply turning on a computer can alter hundreds of files and timestamps. Plugging in a drive can modify it. Remote wiping, automatic updates and cloud synchronisation can destroy evidence within minutes. For this reason the first responder's actions matter greatly: do not browse the device, do not shut it down carelessly, photograph the screen, note what is connected and call a trained examiner.

Ethics and professional conduct

Forensic examiners must be impartial. They report what the evidence shows, including facts that do not support the party that hired them. They keep information confidential, stay within the scope of their authority, and admit the limits of their findings. A credible examiner will say "the evidence is consistent with" rather than overstate a conclusion.

In short, digital forensics combines technical skill, legal awareness and disciplined documentation. Master these fundamentals and every later topic in this series, from imaging to malware analysis, becomes easier to understand.

2. Chain of Custody

Chain of custody is the documented record of who handled a piece of evidence, when, where, why, and what was done to it, from the moment of seizure until it is presented in court or returned. It answers one simple question that every judge and opposing lawyer will ask: how do we know this evidence is the same as what was originally found, and that nobody has changed it?

Why it matters

Even a perfect technical analysis can be thrown out if the chain of custody has gaps. If nobody can explain where a laptop was for three days, the other side can argue it was tampered with, and the court may reduce the weight of the evidence or refuse it entirely. In corporate investigations and disciplinary hearings, a poor chain of custody can also make the findings unfair or unusable. A good chain protects the examiner, the organisation and the person under investigation.

Starting at the scene

Custody begins at the scene. The first responder photographs the area and the devices as found, noting what was on or off, what was connected, and what was on the screen. Each item receives a unique identifier, a label with date and time, and a description including make, model and serial number. Cables, chargers and accessories are recorded too, because they may be needed later. Passwords, notes and sticky notes found nearby are documented as potential evidence.

What the custody form records

A chain of custody form usually contains the case number, the item ID and description, the location and date it was collected, and the name of the person who collected it. After that, every transfer is logged with the name and signature of the person releasing the item, the name and signature of the person receiving it, the date and time, and the reason for the transfer. Any examination, copying or testing is recorded, including which tools were used. Forms must be completed at the time of the event, never later from memory, and corrections are made by a single line and initials, never by erasing.

Packaging and storage

Physical evidence is placed in tamper-evident bags or boxes and sealed, with the seal signed and dated across the edge. Electronic devices are protected from static, heat, moisture and magnetic fields. Mobile phones are isolated from networks with airplane mode, a Faraday bag or a shielded container so they cannot be remotely wiped or receive new data. Items are stored in a secure, access-controlled room or locker, and access to that room is logged.

Transporting evidence

During transport, evidence should stay with an identified person or in a locked container, never left unattended in a vehicle. Long journeys and handovers between agencies must be recorded as clearly as any other transfer. If a courier is used, the courier's details and the tracking information form part of the record.

Digital safeguards: hashes and logs

Digital evidence has an extra layer of protection. When a forensic image is created, its hash value is calculated and recorded on the custody form. Whenever the image is later opened, copied or moved, the hash is calculated again. If the values match, the data is mathematically proven to be unchanged. Evidence storage servers and tools also keep access logs, which can support the paper trail.

Working copies

Examiners work from copies of the image, not the master. The master image is stored securely and used only to create new working copies. Each copy is also hashed and logged. This approach means that even if a working copy is damaged, the original evidence remains safe and available.

Common mistakes to avoid

Typical failures include missing signatures, unexplained time gaps, mixing evidence from different cases, using a personal laptop to examine evidence, leaving a device powered on and connected to the network, and storing items in an unlocked office. Another frequent error is changing the device in order to look at it, such as opening files directly on the original drive.

Practical example

Imagine a manager's laptop is seized after a data leak. The investigator photographs it, seals it in a bag with ID 001, and signs the form. The laptop is handed to the lab examiner, who signs for it, records the time, and stores it in a locked cabinet. The drive is imaged and hashed, and the hash is written on the form. The laptop is resealed and returned to storage. Months later, in a hearing, the examiner can show every step and prove nothing was altered.

Chain of custody is not complicated, but it requires discipline. Record everything, record it immediately, and never leave a gap.

3. Forensic Imaging and Hashing

A forensic image is an exact, bit-for-bit copy of a storage device or partition. Unlike a normal file copy, it includes everything on the drive: active files, deleted files, unallocated space, slack space, file system structures and hidden areas. The image becomes the examiner's working evidence, so the original device can be sealed, stored and left untouched.

Why we image instead of copy

A simple copy and paste captures only visible files and changes access times on the way. It misses deleted data, hidden partitions and system areas, and it cannot prove that nothing was altered. Imaging solves these problems. It preserves the full state of the drive, allows unlimited analysis without risking the original, and lets the examiner verify integrity with hashes at any time.

Write blockers

Before connecting a suspect drive, the examiner uses a write blocker, a device or software layer that allows data to be read but blocks any write command. Without one, merely plugging a drive into a computer can modify timestamps, mount the file system or write system files. Hardware write blockers sit between the drive and the examiner's machine and work independently of the operating system, so they are preferred for court work. Software blockers can be used in emergencies, but they must be tested and documented. The examiner should test the blocker regularly and note the model and firmware used.

Types of acquisition

A physical image copies the entire drive sector by sector, including unallocated areas. A logical image copies only selected partitions or files visible to the file system. A targeted collection gathers specific folders or file types when the full drive is too large, impractical or legally restricted. Live acquisition captures data from a running system, which is necessary when full-disk encryption is active or when volatile data is needed. The method chosen must be explained in the notes.

Image formats

The raw format, often called dd, is a straightforward exact copy with no extra data. It is simple and widely supported but large. The E01 format, from EnCase, adds compression, case information, examiner notes and built-in hash verification. AFF is an open format with similar features. Tools such as FTK Imager, Guymager, dc3dd and Autopsy can create or read these formats. Large images may be split into segments for easier storage.

The imaging process

The basic steps are: connect the source drive through a write blocker; record the make, model and serial number; calculate the source hash if the tool supports it; create the image to a clean, wiped destination drive with enough space; calculate the hash of the image; compare the two hashes; record everything in the log and on the custody form; and store the master image safely. Errors such as bad sectors are noted, because they explain why some data may be missing.

What is hashing?

A hash function takes any amount of data and produces a fixed-length string of characters, called a hash or digest. The same input always gives the same output, but changing even one bit produces a completely different result. It is also designed so that you cannot rebuild the data from the hash. This makes hashes perfect digital fingerprints.

Common hash algorithms

MD5 produces a 128-bit hash and is fast but has known collision weaknesses, where two different inputs can produce the same hash. SHA-1 has similar concerns. SHA-256, part of the SHA-2 family, is currently the preferred standard for forensic integrity. Many examiners record both MD5 and SHA-256 to remain compatible with older tools while using a stronger algorithm.

Uses of hashing in investigations

Hashing proves that an image matches the source and that it has not changed since. It also identifies files: investigators compare file hashes against databases of known files. A set of known operating system and application files can be filtered out to save time, while a set of known malicious or illegal files can be flagged immediately. Hashes can also show that a file found in two locations is identical, even if the names differ.

Verification and good practice

Always verify after imaging, and again whenever evidence is moved or restored. Keep the hash values in the report and the custody record. Use a clean, forensically wiped destination drive so old data is not confused with the evidence. Document tool names and versions, and keep imaging logs. If the hashes do not match, stop, investigate the cause and repeat the process.

Imaging and hashing form the technical backbone of digital forensics. Together they give the examiner a safe working copy and the mathematical proof that it is faithful to the original.

4. Storage, File Systems and Deleted File Recovery

To find evidence, an examiner must understand where and how data is stored. Storage technology, partitioning and file systems all decide what traces remain, where they hide and how they can be recovered.

Storage devices

Hard disk drives store data magnetically on spinning platters. Data stays in place until it is overwritten, which makes recovery of deleted files quite possible. Solid state drives store data in flash memory cells and use a controller to manage them. Features such as wear levelling move data around to extend the life of the drive, and the TRIM command tells the drive which blocks are no longer used so they can be erased in the background. This means deleted data on an SSD may vanish quickly and unpredictably. USB flash drives and memory cards also use flash memory but usually lack TRIM, so recovery is often easier. Optical discs, tapes and cloud storage are other sources.

Sectors, clusters and allocation

A sector is the smallest unit a drive reads or writes, traditionally 512 bytes and now often 4096. File systems group sectors into clusters. A file occupies a whole number of clusters, so the last cluster is rarely full. The unused part is called slack space and may contain leftovers of earlier files. Space currently used by files is allocated; space available for new data is unallocated and may hold deleted content that has not yet been overwritten.

Partitions and boot structures

A drive is divided into partitions. The older MBR scheme supports four primary partitions and drives up to 2 TB. The newer GPT scheme supports far larger drives and many partitions, and stores a backup of its table. Examiners check for hidden, deleted or unusual partitions, since people sometimes hide data in unallocated gaps between partitions. The host protected area and device configuration overlays can also hide data on some older drives.

Common file systems

NTFS is the main Windows file system. It keeps a Master File Table with a record for each file, holding the name, size, timestamps and location of data. Small files may be stored inside the record itself. NTFS also supports alternate data streams, which can hide data attached to a normal file, and a journal that records changes. FAT32 and exFAT are common on removable media and cameras and are simple, with a file allocation table that links clusters. ext4 is the usual Linux file system and uses inodes to store file metadata. APFS and HFS+ are used by Apple devices.

Timestamps

Most file systems record when a file was modified, accessed, changed or created (often called MACB times). These are vital for timelines but can be misleading: copying a file may change some times, and operating systems update access times in different ways. Examiners interpret timestamps carefully and confirm them with other sources.

How deletion works

When a user deletes a file and empties the Recycle Bin, the system normally just marks its directory entry or record as unused and its clusters as available. The actual data remains on the disk until new data overwrites it. That is why deleted files can often be recovered, and why overwriting a drive many times is needed for real erasure.

Recovery methods

File system recovery uses surviving metadata, such as old MFT records, directory entries or inodes, to restore the file name, path, dates and content. It gives the best results because the original information is intact. File carving ignores the file system and scans raw data for known file signatures. For example, JPEG images start with specific bytes and end with a recognisable marker, so a carving tool can cut out everything between them. Carving can find files after formatting, but it usually loses names and dates and struggles with fragmented files.

Factors that affect recovery

Recovery is more likely when the drive was little used after deletion, the file was small and unfragmented, and the device is a traditional hard drive. It is less likely on SSDs with TRIM, on heavily used systems, on encrypted drives without the key, and after secure wiping. Examiners also check other sources: the Recycle Bin, Volume Shadow Copies, backups, temporary files, thumbnails, and cloud sync folders often retain copies of deleted files.

Good practice

Always perform recovery on a forensic image, never the original. Record the tool used, its version and the settings. Hash each recovered file, note whether it was recovered by file system or carving, and validate that the file opens correctly. Keep partial files, since even a fragment can be useful.

Knowing how drives and file systems behave turns deleted data from a mystery into a recoverable resource.

5. Windows and Linux Forensics

Operating systems quietly record a great deal about what users and programs do. Windows and Linux dominate desktops and servers, and each leaves its own trail of artifacts. Knowing where to look saves hours of work.

Windows: the Registry

The Windows Registry is a database of settings stored in hive files such as SYSTEM, SOFTWARE, SAM, SECURITY and each user's NTUSER.DAT. It reveals installed programs, user accounts, network names, autostart entries used for persistence, recently opened files (RecentDocs, MRU lists), typed paths and searches. It also records USB devices connected, including the serial number, first and last connection times, which is central in data theft investigations. Keys carry a last-write time that helps with timelines.

Windows: logs and execution evidence

Event Logs record logons and logoffs, failed logins, service installations, scheduled tasks, PowerShell activity and policy changes. Specific event IDs, such as 4624 for successful logon and 4625 for failed logon, are widely used. Prefetch files record that a program ran, how many times and when, plus files it loaded. Amcache and ShimCache record programs that existed or executed on the system. UserAssist stores programs launched through the graphical interface, and the BAM key shows recent execution times.

Windows: user activity

LNK shortcut files and Jump Lists show files and folders that a user opened, including those on removable drives or network shares. Shellbags show folders viewed, even if later deleted. The Recycle Bin keeps the original name and path of deleted files. Thumbnail caches may hold pictures that no longer exist. Browser data, email and the Windows search index add more. The pagefile and hibernation file may hold fragments of memory. Volume Shadow Copies preserve earlier versions of files and the registry, allowing examiners to see how things looked in the past.

Windows: persistence and attacker traces

Attackers keep access through Run keys, services, scheduled tasks, WMI subscriptions and startup folders. Examiners check these locations, review the creation of new accounts and look for unusual executables in temporary or user-writable folders. The NTFS journal and MFT help show when suspicious files appeared and were removed.

Linux: logs

Linux stores most evidence in plain text and configuration files. Under /var/log, auth.log or secure records logins, SSH sessions and sudo commands. Syslog or messages capture system events, and the systemd journal holds service logs. Web server and application logs show access and errors. Files such as wtmp, btmp and lastlog record login history and failed attempts, and the last and lastb commands read them.

Linux: users, history and persistence

The files /etc/passwd and /etc/shadow list accounts and password hashes; look for new users, unexpected UID 0 accounts or accounts with login shells that should not have them. Each user's .bash_history shows typed commands, though it can be cleared or disabled. The ~/.ssh folder holds authorized keys, which attackers add for persistent access, and known_hosts, which shows machines the user connected to. Persistence appears in crontab entries, systemd service and timer units, init scripts and shell profile files such as .bashrc.

Linux: file system evidence

On ext4, inodes hold modified, accessed and changed times; deletion times may also be present. Directories such as /tmp, /var/tmp and /dev/shm often contain tools dropped by attackers. Examiners also check for files with the SUID bit set, recently modified binaries, unusual hidden files and package manager logs showing installed software.

Cross-checking and cautions

Attackers often delete logs and history, so use several sources to confirm events. Compare timestamps across logs, file systems and memory, and remember time zones and clock settings. Always work on images or read-only mounts so you do not change the evidence.

Whether the system is Windows or Linux, the method is the same: find who did what, with which account, at what time, and prove it with more than one artifact.

6. Memory and Network Forensics

Disk analysis shows what was stored, but memory and network analysis show what was happening. Together they reveal live attacks, hidden malware and the movement of data.

Why memory matters

Random access memory holds everything the computer is currently using: running processes, loaded libraries, open files, network connections, clipboard contents, command history, decrypted data and encryption keys. Some malware lives only in memory and never writes to disk, so it leaves no file to find. Memory is also where passwords and keys may appear in readable form. Because RAM is volatile, it must be collected early, before shutting down or rebooting.

Acquiring memory

Memory is captured from a live system with tools such as FTK Imager, DumpIt, WinPmem or LiME on Linux. The tool should run from external media to avoid changing the system more than needed, and the output should be written to another drive. The examiner records the time, the tool and its hash. Capturing memory slightly changes the system, so the action and its reason must be documented. Hibernation files and crash dumps can also hold memory contents.

Analysing memory

The Volatility framework is the standard open-source tool. Typical steps are to list processes and their parent-child relationships, view command lines, list network connections, and inspect loaded modules. Useful plugins find hidden or terminated processes, extract registry hives, dump suspicious executables and show injected code. Red flags include system process names running from the wrong folder, a document application launching a command shell, processes with no parent, unsigned modules, and memory regions that are both writable and executable, which is a sign of code injection.

Network forensics: sources

Network evidence comes from full packet captures (PCAP files), flow records such as NetFlow, and logs from firewalls, routers, intrusion detection systems, proxies, DNS servers, VPNs and web servers. Packet captures hold the most detail but need large storage; flow records and logs show who talked to whom, when and how much, with far less space.

Network forensics: analysis

Wireshark is the standard tool for reading packets, with Zeek and tcpdump also widely used. The examiner filters by IP address, port, protocol and time. Common findings include unusual outbound connections to unknown addresses, large uploads suggesting data theft, repeated connections at fixed intervals (beaconing) that indicate malware calling its controller, port scans, brute-force login attempts and suspicious DNS requests such as very long or random-looking domain names. Files transferred over unencrypted protocols can often be reconstructed from the capture.

Encryption and its limits

Most traffic is now encrypted, so content is often unreadable. Even so, metadata remains valuable: IP addresses, server names, certificate details, timing, packet sizes and volume. Combined with endpoint logs and memory analysis, metadata can show what happened without breaking encryption.

Linking memory and network evidence

The strongest findings tie both together. A connection seen in network logs at a certain time can be matched to the process that owned the socket in memory. A suspicious process in memory can lead to the remote address in the packet capture and then to other infected machines. This correlation turns scattered clues into a complete story.

Legal and practical points

Capturing network traffic can expose private communications, so it needs authority, a clear scope and secure handling. Record where the capture point was placed, since that decides what you could see. Time synchronisation across devices is essential for correlating events.

Quick checklist

On a live incident: record the time and system state, capture memory, save network connections and running processes, preserve firewall and DNS logs before they rotate, then image the disks. Keep notes of every command you run, because each one leaves traces. Afterwards, analyse memory for processes and injected code, review the network data for outside contact, and write down which finding supports which conclusion.

Collect memory first, preserve network logs before they rotate, and always connect what you see in one source to evidence in another.

7. Mobile, Browser and Email Forensics

Most people carry their lives on a phone, browse the web all day and communicate by email. These three areas often decide the outcome of an investigation because they show what a person did, where they were and who they talked to.

Why mobile devices matter

A smartphone holds calls, text and chat messages, contacts, photos and videos, calendar entries, notes, app data, saved passwords, Wi-Fi history and location records. Sensors and apps can show where the device was at a certain time, how many steps were taken, or when the screen was unlocked. Because the phone is nearly always with its owner, it is one of the best witnesses available.

Handling a phone safely

Phones can be wiped remotely, receive new messages that overwrite data, or lock themselves again. The first step is to isolate the device from networks using airplane mode, a Faraday bag or a shielded box, while keeping it charged. Photograph the screen, record the model and whether it is locked, and never guess passcodes, since repeated failures may erase the data. Document everything in the chain of custody.

Acquisition levels

A logical acquisition pulls data the operating system will hand over, such as contacts and messages. A file system acquisition copies the full folder structure, including app databases and some deleted records. A physical acquisition captures the raw memory and gives the most data, but it is harder on modern encrypted phones. Commercial tools such as Cellebrite, Magnet AXIOM and Oxygen help, and examiners must test and record which method worked. Cloud backups, with proper legal authority, can provide another copy.

Mobile challenges and artifacts

Strong encryption, secure chips, frequent updates and new app versions make mobile work difficult, so tools must be kept current. Much of the useful data sits in SQLite databases, which can still hold deleted rows in free pages and write-ahead logs. Examiners look at message databases, call logs, photo metadata (EXIF) with GPS coordinates, location history, installed apps, and notification and usage records.

Browser forensics

Browsers keep history, bookmarks, downloads, cache, cookies, saved form entries, autofill data, session information and extensions. Chrome, Edge and Firefox store most of this in SQLite files within the user profile. History shows visited pages with timestamps and visit counts; search terms often appear in the URL. The download list shows what files were fetched and where they were saved. The cache may contain copies of pages and images. Private browsing leaves fewer traces, but traces can still appear in memory, DNS cache and system logs.

Interpreting browser evidence

An entry in the history does not always prove the user typed it: pages can redirect, load in the background or be opened by malware. Examiners look at visit types, referring pages and surrounding activity to decide whether the visit was deliberate. Timestamps in browsers often use special formats such as WebKit time, which must be converted correctly, and time zones checked.

Email forensics: headers

Every email carries a header with technical details. The Received lines list each server the message passed through, usually read from bottom to top, which reveals the true path. Other fields include the sender, reply-to address, message ID, date and authentication results. A mismatch between the displayed sender and the real origin is a classic sign of spoofing or phishing.

Email authentication and storage

SPF checks whether the sending server is allowed to send for the domain, DKIM verifies a cryptographic signature that proves the message was not altered, and DMARC sets the policy when these checks fail. Results appear in the header. Emails are stored locally in files such as PST and OST for Outlook, MBOX for many other clients, or on mail servers and in webmail. Deleted messages may remain in the recoverable items folder, in the file's unused space, or on the server backup.

Attachments and links

Attachments should be hashed and, if suspicious, analysed in a safe environment. Links in phishing emails are examined without clicking them directly, checking the real destination, domain age and redirects. Email evidence also reveals who knew what and when, which matters in fraud and policy cases.

In every case, extract data from forensic copies, convert timestamps carefully and confirm what you find with a second source, such as server logs or the other party's device.

8. Malware Forensics and Timeline Reconstruction

When a system is attacked, two questions matter most: what did the malicious software do, and in what order did events happen? Malware forensics answers the first and timeline reconstruction answers the second.

What malware forensics aims to find

The goals are to identify the malware, work out how it entered, learn what it did on the system, find how it stays after reboot, discover what it communicated with, and produce indicators that help defend other machines. This supports containment, cleaning and prevention, as well as legal action.

Safe handling

Malware must be handled in an isolated lab, ideally in a virtual machine with snapshots and no connection to real networks or shared folders. Samples are stored in password-protected archives and renamed so they cannot run by accident. Record the hash of every sample before analysis. Never analyse malware on a normal work computer.

Static analysis

Static analysis examines the file without running it. The examiner calculates hashes and checks them against public reputation services, reads printable strings that may reveal addresses, commands or messages, and inspects the file header, sections and imported functions. Imports such as network, registry or process injection functions hint at behaviour. High entropy often signals packing or encryption, which hides the real code. Disassemblers and decompilers can then be used by skilled analysts.

Dynamic analysis

Dynamic analysis runs the sample in a controlled sandbox and watches it. Monitoring tools capture files created or changed, registry modifications, new processes, services and scheduled tasks, and network traffic such as DNS requests and connections to remote servers. Taking snapshots before and after execution makes the changes easy to compare. Some malware detects virtual machines and stays quiet, so results may need repeating with different settings.

Indicators of compromise

The findings are turned into indicators of compromise: file hashes, file names and paths, registry keys, mutex names, domain names, IP addresses, user-agent strings and unique network patterns. Defenders can search for these across the network, in logs and with security tools. Behaviour-based rules, such as YARA signatures, describe the malware's features more flexibly than a single hash.

What is a timeline?

A timeline puts events from many sources into one ordered list, showing cause and effect. It can reveal the first entry point, the moment of compromise, the attacker's steps and when data left the network. It is also useful for non-malware cases, such as showing what a user did on a given day.

Sources for a timeline

Typical sources include file system timestamps (modified, accessed, changed, created), the NTFS MFT and journal, Windows event logs, registry key times, Prefetch, browser history, email dates, application logs, firewall and proxy logs, and memory artifacts. The more independent sources, the stronger the timeline.

Building and reading the timeline

Tools like Plaso with log2timeline collect artifacts from an image into a single super timeline, which can then be filtered and viewed with tools such as Timesketch. A practical approach is to start with a known time, such as when an alert fired or a file was opened, and work outward in both directions. Look for clusters of activity, new executables, unusual logons and anything that happens just before or after the key event.

Pitfalls

Time zones and daylight saving changes cause confusion, so convert everything to one standard, usually UTC. Computer clocks can be wrong or deliberately changed. Some timestamps update when files are copied or opened, and attackers can alter them using timestomping, so the MFT and journal should be compared to spot inconsistencies. A single timestamp is a clue, not proof.

Putting it together

Suppose a user opens a phishing attachment at 09:12. The timeline shows a new file at 09:12, a PowerShell command at 09:13, a connection to an unknown server at 09:14, a new scheduled task at 09:20 and large uploads at midnight. Malware analysis of the dropped file explains each step. Together they give a clear narrative backed by evidence.

Document each step, mark which findings are facts and which are interpretation, and keep the timeline reproducible.

9. Forensic Tools, Validation and Anti-Forensics

Forensic conclusions are only as trustworthy as the tools and methods behind them. A good examiner knows what the tools do, proves they work and understands how people try to defeat them.

Categories of tools

Acquisition tools such as FTK Imager, Guymager and dc3dd create images. Full analysis suites such as Autopsy, EnCase, FTK and X-Ways parse file systems, recover deleted files, search keywords and build reports. Memory tools such as Volatility examine RAM, Wireshark and Zeek handle network data, and Plaso builds timelines. Mobile tools such as Cellebrite and Magnet AXIOM extract phone data, while registry viewers, SQLite browsers and hex editors help examine specific artifacts.

Open source or commercial?

Commercial tools tend to offer support, polished reports and broad device coverage, but they cost money. Open-source tools are free, transparent and flexible, and their methods can be inspected, but they may need more skill. Many labs combine both, and using two tools to cross-check the same data increases confidence.

Why validation is essential

Tools can contain bugs, misread new file formats or miss artifacts after software updates. If an examiner testifies based on a faulty tool, the case and their reputation can collapse. Validation means demonstrating that a tool does what it claims in the conditions in which it is used.

How to validate

Test tools on datasets with known contents, such as NIST CFTT reference images and public practice images, and compare the output with what you know should be there. Verify that imaging tools produce matching hashes and that analysis tools show consistent results across versions. Re-test after every significant update. Keep records of versions, test results and any limits found. Verify surprising findings manually, for instance by checking raw data in a hex editor.

Standards and legal acceptance

Courts look for methods that are tested, peer-reviewed, have a known error rate and are generally accepted. Following standards such as ISO 27037 for evidence handling and accredited lab practices helps. Examiners should be able to explain in plain language what a tool did and why its results can be trusted.

Anti-forensics techniques

Anti-forensics means actions taken to prevent, hide or mislead an investigation. Common examples are secure deletion and wiping tools that overwrite data, full-disk and file encryption, steganography that hides data inside images or audio, and timestamp manipulation. Others include clearing event logs and shell history, using portable or in-memory tools that leave few traces, renaming files or changing extensions, and using anonymising networks.

Detecting anti-forensics

Anti-forensic activity itself leaves clues. Wiping tools may leave their own prefetch entries, registry traces or recently used lists. Gaps in logs, sudden log clearing events and mismatched timestamps are suspicious. Files whose header does not match the extension can be spotted with signature analysis. Large files of high entropy with no explanation may be encrypted containers. Memory analysis and network logs can show activity that disk cleaning cannot remove.

Wider challenges

Investigators face huge data volumes, so triage and keyword filtering are needed to focus on what matters. Cloud services place data in other countries, raising jurisdiction and cooperation issues. Encrypted phones and drives may be unreadable without the key. Internet of Things devices, vehicles and wearables use unusual formats. Privacy laws require careful limits on what is collected. Rapid technology change means constant learning.

Staying effective

Keep tools updated and tested, document your methods, work in teams to peer-review findings, follow current research, and practise on sample cases. Be honest about limitations: say what was not found or could not be recovered.

Reliable tools, tested methods and awareness of evasion tricks make findings stand up under questioning.

10. Practical Investigation Case, Advanced Concepts and Revision

This final article puts everything together. First you will follow a realistic case from start to finish, then look at advanced topics, and finally review the key points to remember.

The case: suspected data theft

A company reports that confidential price lists appeared with a competitor shortly after a sales employee resigned. Management suspects the employee copied files from a company laptop. The investigator is asked to establish facts without assuming guilt.

Step 1: Authority and preservation

The investigator first confirms the legal authority to examine the laptop, such as company policy and written approval. The laptop is photographed, labelled, sealed in a tamper-evident bag and logged on a chain of custody form. It is not switched on at the office. Related accounts, such as email and cloud storage, are noted so logs can be preserved before they expire.

Step 2: Acquisition

In the lab, the drive is connected through a hardware write blocker and imaged to a clean destination drive. The source and image are hashed with SHA-256 and the values match. The original is resealed and stored, and all work continues on a copy. The hash values, tool versions and times are recorded.

Step 3: Examination

The examiner opens the copy in a forensic suite and starts with the questions: what files were accessed, was anything copied out, and was anything deleted? The registry shows a USB flash drive connected twice in the final week, with its make and serial number and first and last connection times. LNK files and Jump Lists show that files from the price list folder were opened from a drive letter that matches the USB device. The Recycle Bin and unallocated space show several of the files were later deleted from the laptop, and some are recovered by carving.

Step 4: Other sources

Browser history shows visits to a personal webmail site and a file-sharing service on the day before resignation, and the download and upload lists record file names. Email headers and sent items show two messages with attachments sent to a personal address. Company server logs confirm that the employee's account accessed the price-list share outside normal hours.

Step 5: Timeline

All findings are merged into a timeline using one time standard. It shows: the share accessed at 18:40, files opened at 18:45, the USB connected at 18:52, files copied between 18:52 and 19:05, the USB removed at 19:06, files deleted at 19:10, and the email sent at 19:20. Each entry is supported by at least two independent artifacts.

Step 6: Reporting

The report states the scope, the methods, the tools and versions, the evidence examined, the findings and their limits. It separates facts from opinion, uses clear language, and includes hashes, the timeline and the custody record. It also states what was not found, for example that no evidence shows what happened to the USB drive afterwards, and notes that the evidence is consistent with copying but cannot by itself prove who decided to use the data. The examiner remains neutral and leaves legal decisions to others.

Advanced concepts

Cloud forensics deals with data held in online services, where logs and evidence depend on the provider, jurisdiction and legal process. Live response and incident response at scale collect key artifacts from many machines quickly using endpoint tools. Memory-resident and fileless threats need memory and behaviour analysis. Encrypted and mobile devices need specialised tools and often legal processes to obtain keys or backups. Anti-forensics demands cross-checking sources. Automation, machine learning and AI-assisted triage can sort huge amounts of data, but their results must still be verified by a human. Other growing areas include IoT, vehicle and wearable forensics, and digital evidence from social media and messaging apps.

Revision: the key points

Preserve first and never work on the original. Use write blockers, create forensic images and verify them with hashes. Keep a complete chain of custody and record every action. Follow the order of volatility and collect memory and network data early. Understand file systems, because deleted data often remains. Know the main Windows and Linux artifacts, and the value of mobile, browser and email evidence. Correlate several sources and build timelines in one time standard. Validate your tools, expect anti-forensics, and report objectively, with facts clearly separated from opinion.

The forensic process in order

Identify, preserve, collect, examine, analyse, report. If you remember this sequence and the reason behind each step, you can handle most investigations.

Self-test questions

Why do we hash an image? What is the order of volatility? What does a write blocker do? Where would you find evidence of a USB device on Windows? How can you tell if a log was cleared? Why must tools be validated? Try answering these in your own words before checking the earlier articles.
